## 1. The target format

CrossWorld Chronicles is evolving from narrated image collages toward **animated historical docudrama**. The creator already has a working AI production system that can generate narration, dialogue scenes, action, multi-character interactions, visuals, and production assets. The current problem is story construction, continuity, and how narration and scenes combine into one compelling video.

The intended audience experience:

1. **Cold open / trailer-like hook.** Begin with a dramatic event or contradiction. Intercut brief visual previews of later consequences. Do not give away every answer.
2. **Minimal orientation.** Establish time, place, central figure, and stakes only after the hook creates a reason to care.
3. **Narrator hand-off.** The narrator supplies context that characters cannot naturally explain to each other, then hands over to a scene at the moment a decision, confrontation, discovery, or reversal must happen.
4. **Played scene.** Characters want different, concrete things. Their words and physical actions change the situation. The scene must not be a static exchange of exposition.
5. **Narrator consequence.** After the scene, the narrator explains the consequence the scene itself cannot fully communicate, then points toward the next pressure or decision.
6. **Causal escalation.** Each segment happens because of what came before, but also causes a new problem, cost, opportunity, or misunderstanding.
7. **Payoff.** The ending answers the central dramatic question and pays off at least one earlier setup. Any open question that remains must be deliberately framed as unresolved history, not an accidental dropped thread.

This should feel contemporary and accessible, not like a slow academic documentary. It must still respect the historical record. Dialogue can be reconstructed, but must never be falsely presented as a verbatim historical exchange.

### What a successful segment does

A segment must perform a story function. It might reveal a new fact that changes a character's options; force a choice between two costs; make an existing plan fail; change who trusts or controls whom; introduce an obstacle that causes the next scene; reframe an earlier event; or make a larger system personally consequential.

A segment that only repeats facts from the narrator, contains generic banter, or ends with everyone in the same position should be removed, combined, or redesigned.

## 3. Synthesis: transferable writing principles

These principles are supported by the inspected material and should inform original writing, not replace the existing rulebook.

### Principle 1: Tell one causal story, not a list of true events

Historical research supplies facts; story architecture determines why each fact appears here and now.

Before drafting lines, define the protagonist or central group; the goal they pursue; the obstacle that makes it difficult; the central dramatic question; the cost of failure; the turning points; and what changes at the ending.

Each segment needs a causal relation to the previous segment. Use the existing BUT/THEREFORE model in docs/STORY_ENGINE.md. Do not add a separate competing beat system unless a test demonstrates a real gap.

### Principle 2: The cold open is a promise, not a summary

A good cold open does three jobs: gives the viewer a concrete situation; exposes a contradiction or danger; and promises an answer that the video will earn.

A hook can tease later images, but the images must correspond to real planned story beats. Avoid a random montage of exciting shots that never receive a payoff.

### Principle 3: Narration and dialogue perform different jobs

**Narration is good for:**
- chronology and location;
- facts no character could naturally know;
- explaining a wider political, economic, scientific, or military system;
- summarizing time jumps;
- connecting consequences between scenes;
- correcting a misunderstanding or giving the audience the minimum context needed.

**Scenes are good for:**
- immediate decisions;
- conflicts of interest;
- discovering bad news;
- competing plans;
- fear, loyalty, betrayal, ambition, embarrassment, or grief;
- visible choices with consequences;
- a character withholding or distorting information.

If the narrator explains a conflict and characters then repeat it line by line, the scene is redundant. The scene should add a reaction, decision, disagreement, new fact, or cost that narration alone cannot provide.

### Principle 4: A scene is a change in the situation

A scene header should state where and when it happens, who is physically present, whose POV governs it, what that person wants right now, which other person wants something incompatible, what value is at stake, what turns the scene, and how the scene exits.

A conversation is not automatically dramatic. Conflict might be indirect: a character refuses to answer, hides information, uses a resource as leverage, or performs an action that forces another character to respond.

Keep the existing SC-01..SC-10 and DW-01..DW-13 rules. The scene must change the charge of the value at stake, and the next segment must inherit its consequence.

### Principle 5: A fact becomes useful when it changes a choice

Do not insert facts merely because they were found in the research dossier. Ask:
- Does this fact constrain a plan?
- Does it alter a character's understanding?
- Does it reveal a motive?
- Does it make an earlier choice look different?
- Does it create a risk or opportunity?
- Is this the moment the audience needs it?

A number needs a narrative reason. Army size matters when it changes a commander's decision; a business figure matters when it changes a deal; a date matters when it creates a deadline or explains why two events are connected.

### Principle 6: The larger system must remain tied to personal stakes

The Wyatt Earp example moves from a local crime to economic and political forces because the case becomes stuck. The Food That Built America links inventions to urbanization, infrastructure, consumer trust, and regulation. The Napoleon account links strategy to terrain, supply, disease, morale, and diplomacy.

Use a system-level explanation when it answers an active story question. Then return to a person who must act within that system.

### Principle 7: Build reversals from established constraints

A reversal should not appear because the script needs a surprise. Plant the constraint first. A delayed supply train, a rival's incentive, an unreliable witness, a changing alliance, a legal rule, or a resource shortage can become the cause of a later failure.

Use setup/payoff and open-loop tracking already present in the Story Engine. Do not add random twists that contradict the historical record.

### Principle 8: A win can create the next problem

The most useful progressions in these sources are not simple success ladders. A deal creates distrust; a popular product creates copycats and quality-control problems; a major advance increases logistical exposure. Design each success with its natural next cost.

### Principle 9: Dialogue should be specific, not merely period-flavoured

Historical-sounding language alone does not make a scene believable. Dialogue should reveal what a person wants, knows, fears, hides, or refuses to accept. Give each participant a different strategy.

Avoid characters explaining shared background for the audience, sequences of clean question-and-answer exchanges, textbook speeches, interchangeable character voices, or famous people speaking invented lines presented as authentic quotes.

Use the current dialogue-scene rules and make exposition a weapon, a deadline, a discovery, or a disagreement when it belongs in dialogue.

### Principle 10: Historical certainty and dramatic certainty are different

For each scene, separately track:
- **Verified event:** the underlying event is documented.
- **Documented quotation:** the words are supported by a source.
- **Reconstruction:** the scene or conversation is invented to dramatize a verified event or plausible situation.
- **Interpretation:** a reasoned explanation of motives or causation.
- **Unknown/contested:** the evidence does not support a single confident statement.

A reconstruction may be emotionally persuasive without being a record of what was said. Mark it honestly in the production script and disclosure beat according to the repository's existing historical-integrity and episode-rulebook gates.

## 4. Original example for CrossWorld Chronicles

The following is an **original instructional example**, not a transcript from any documentary. The dialogue is invented solely to illustrate scene structure. It must not be represented in a video as a documented historical conversation. Historical claims in any produced version must have claim IDs and supporting sources.

### Dramatic premise

A commander wants a quick political victory, but the opponent refuses to fight on the commander's timetable. The advancing army is beginning to experience supply problems. The immediate scene concerns whether to continue the advance or pause.

### Cold-open structure

**VISUAL TEASE:** boots sinking into mud; an exhausted courier arriving; a map with a long route; a commander staring at an empty supply ledger; a distant city appearing through smoke.

**NARRATOR — original template:**
"The army has crossed deep into enemy territory. The capital is closer than it has ever been. But the victory the commander expected still hasn't arrived — and every day he waits, the problem grows harder to solve."

**Purpose:** gives an objective, contradiction, and consequence. It avoids opening with a biography or a long list of dates.

### Hand-off into a scene

**NARRATOR:**
"At headquarters, another report arrives. It doesn't announce a defeat. It reveals something more dangerous: the timetable is breaking."

**Scene header**
- Place: temporary field headquarters, night.
- Present: commander, quartermaster, ambitious general.
- POV: commander.
- Commander wants: to keep the advance moving without appearing uncertain.
- Quartermaster wants: approval to slow down until supplies catch up.
- General wants: to continue, believing delay will give the opponent time.
- Value: confidence/control.
- Opening charge: commander appears in control.
- Turning beat: quartermaster reveals that supplies required for the next stage cannot arrive on schedule.
- Exit: the commander orders a limited advance but demands a new plan, creating a compromise that satisfies nobody fully.

**Dialogue — invented for this teaching example:**

QUARTERMASTER: "The forward depots are behind schedule."

COMMANDER: "By how much?"

QUARTERMASTER: "Enough that the next move spends what we haven't received."

GENERAL: "Then take what we need from the villages."

QUARTERMASTER: "That works once. Not across another hundred miles."

COMMANDER: "You were given the road and the timetable."

QUARTERMASTER: "I was given both. Neither one makes the wagons faster."

**Physical beat:** the commander starts to mark the route on the map, then stops before drawing the next arrow.

COMMANDER: "Give me two plans. One for moving. One for staying alive if the road fails."

**NARRATOR — consequence and next pressure:**
"The advance continues, but now there are two plans instead of one. The enemy doesn't have to defeat the army in battle. It only has to keep the army moving for long enough."

**Why it works structurally**
- The narrator introduces a pressure rather than narrating the whole scene.
- Each character has a distinct objective.
- The dialogue contains a real disagreement, not shared exposition.
- A physical action carries the emotional turn.
- The commander makes a choice, but the choice creates a new problem.
- The narrator adds the consequence rather than restating the dialogue.
- The example avoids claiming that this meeting or these exact words happened historically.

**Production note:** replace the hypothetical situation with verified facts from the episode's research dossier. Keep invented dialogue labelled as reconstruction. Do not use this example as evidence for historical facts.

## 5. How to turn reference observations into few-shot examples

Do not store long raw subtitles inside prompts. Build an annotated library of about 6–12 compact original examples, selected to cover different functions rather than several nearly identical scenes.

Each example should record:
1. A source reference and access status.
2. The narrative role: hook, setup, escalation, reversal, consequence, payoff, or ending.
3. The context immediately before the scene.
4. What the narrator needs to explain.
5. The scene contract: POV, conflicting wants, value, turn, exit.
6. The causal result that leads into the next segment.
7. The transferable technique, stated in original words.
8. Whether the analysis is based on readable text, only page metadata, or an uninspected subtitle file.
9. A note that full copyrighted transcript text is not stored.

### Recommended example coverage
1. Cold open that reframes a known historical event.
2. Narrator-to-action hand-off.
3. Two characters with incompatible objectives.
4. A character holds back information and uses leverage.
5. A failed plan caused by an established constraint.
6. A local event opens into a wider economic/political explanation.
7. Parallel storylines connected by one central theme.
8. Apparent victory creates a more difficult second act.
9. An uncertainty-aware reconstruction of a historical decision.
10. An ending that pays off a visual or factual setup from the opening.

A few strong, well-annotated examples are more valuable than a large pile of raw text with no explanation of why each passage works.

## 7. Evaluation plan

Compare the current generator and a proposed revised version on the **same** researched topic and the same required runtime.

Suggested test topics:
- Napoleon's invasion of Russia (military campaign and logistics).
- A political power struggle (alliances, threats, conflicting incentives).
- A business rivalry (invention, public failure, competition, regulation).
- A scientific breakthrough (hypothesis, experiment, failure, consequence).
- A historical disaster (warning signs, decisions, cascading effects, accountability).

Score both versions, each with an evidence note:
1. The central question is clear and answered at the end.
2. The first 15–30 seconds open a strong question or contradiction.
3. The opening tease receives a payoff.
4. Each segment has a causal connection to the previous one.
5. A scene exists because characters need to decide, confront, discover, or act.
6. The dialogue reveals conflicting wants without textbook exposition.
7. The scene changes the value or situation at stake.
8. The narrator adds new context rather than paraphrasing dialogue.
9. Historical claims are sourced and reconstructed dialogue is not misrepresented as fact.
10. Stakes and complications escalate without arbitrary twists.
11. The ending resolves the central question and pays off a setup.
12. The result can be visualized as animation without relying entirely on static illustrations.

Keep an example of each version, the scores, and the reason for each score. Do not claim success merely because the prompt became longer or the script became more dramatic-sounding.
