# Dialogue craft for historical figures (layer L3, stage 2)

Built from jtydhr88/screenwriting-skills, page `sw-dialogue` (MIT, commit `51115f18d1`; McKee's Dialogue, Walter, Hicks, Lu Jun,
Egri, Snyder, screwball conventions). Rewritten in English for docudrama under RW-50
([docs/SKILL_LAYERS.md](../../../docs/SKILL_LAYERS.md)). Upstream Chinese text: `UPSTREAM_SKILL.md`; seven beat-by-beat
scene analyses: `reference.md`. Our scene structure and rules: skill `dialogue-scenes`; registers and few-shot examples:
skill `narration-script` (DW-11).

## The one idea: a line is an action

McKee: dialogue is action through words. Under every line a character wants something and uses a tactic to get it
(pleading, testing, mocking, stalling). Write the tactic first, as a verb, then the words. If you cannot name the action
under a line, the line is decoration; cut it. "Dialogue problems are story problems": a line that will not come right
usually means the scene has no real opposing want (back to the header).

## Three layers in every line

- **Said**: the words, their vocabulary and grammar.
- **Unsaid**: what the speaker knows and hides on purpose; the viewer should feel it.
- **Unsayable**: what the speaker does not know about himself; it shows only under pressure, in a choice.

A scene where everything is said is dead ("on the nose"). The two allowed on-the-nose lines are "What do you want?" and
"Are you all right?"; everything else stays on the surface topic while the real subject works underneath (SC-10).

## Exposition as ammunition

Give the viewer only the facts the story needs, and only when a character would use them as a weapon or pry them out.
Characters never tell each other what both know ("As you know, we have been here since October"): that is the narrator's
job, or a line used as a jab ("Two months in this ditch and you still flinch"). The narrator carries dates, numbers and
background; heroes carry stakes. Ration roughly one dialogue-borne fact per two or three beats; the knowledge-state table
(K/K, K/U, U/U) is in skill `dialogue-scenes`.

## Historical voice without pastiche

- **Vocabulary from the record**: letters, diaries and memoirs of the period give the nouns and idioms (Tommy, Fritz,
  "the line", "the other side"). Use them lightly; one period word per line is plenty.
- **No anachronism**: no modern idiom ("okay" before its time, "deal with it", "reach out"), no modern concept in a period
  mouth. The register lint catches AI words (RG rules); the period check is yours and the critic's.
- **Real quotations stay short and sourced**: a recorded sentence goes in as spoken only if the claim map holds it; a
  paraphrase in the hero's mouth is allowed when it is plausible and labelled `reasoned` in the segment.
- **Not English? Still English.** A German or French hero speaks English for the US viewer, with one or two native words
  where they are understood from context ("Frohe Weihnachten"), never a fake accent spelled out.
- **Different people, different grammar**: an officer uses full sentences and the conditional; a private uses fragments,
  slang and swears off screen; a farmer uses concrete images. Names and verbs show what a man knows (McKee: "a nail" against
  "a tenpenny finishing nail").

## Short lines for our clips

Every hero line is spoken inside one video clip (D-131, one line per clip, VL-02), so long turns are expensive and risky.
Default to short, active lines; a scene may hold one longer turn at its peak (SC-09 caps the length; BP-72, BP-73). If a
line runs long, split it with an action beat or a reaction from the listener. Put the key word at the **end** of the line
(the periodic sentence): it gives the other speaker the cue and the voice the stress.

## Six tasks and four flaws (McKee), as a checklist

Each line should do at least one task: perform an inner action; build the scene to its turn; reveal information or
attitude; mark the speaker as unique; hold attention; stay true to the character. Flaws to remove:
- **Credibility**: lines that state feelings precisely ("I feel betrayed by command"), excuses passed off as motives.
- **Language**: clichés (write the first phrase, then write five more and keep the fifth), neutral words anyone could say,
  ornate lines that show off the writer.
- **Content**: on the nose; inner monologue spoken aloud; two-handers that go stiff (bring in a third thing: a letter, a
  football, the officer's watch).
- **Design**: repeated beats; the key word in the middle of a line; a speech with no new information in each sentence.

## Silence, pause, argument

- **Silence is the most economical line**: a held look, a man who keeps cleaning his rifle (the scripted silence beat).
- Pauses are earned: tighten before the turn, breathe after it.
- **Argument** (Walter's principle): no two characters fully agree on screen; even polite disagreement keeps tension.
- No ellipsis chains, no exclamation marks, no stage parentheticals in the line itself (`intent` and the performance stage
  carry delivery).

## Tests before handing on

1. **Cover the names** (Snyder): can you tell who speaks each line? If not, rewrite with the speech card (DW-11).
2. **Swap test**: give a line to another hero; if it still works, it is a generic line.
3. **Under the line**: name the verb of every line.
4. **Ammunition**: is any fact recited between people who both know it? Cut or weaponise it.
5. **Clip fit**: does each line fit one clip with its tail (VL-06)? Split long lines.
6. **Period**: one modern word breaks the docudrama; check every idiom.

## Comedy inside a grim story

Humour comes from people under pressure behaving like people: the enemy who asks for the football score, the cook's
complaint about the Christmas pudding. Clarity, timing (the laugh word last), understatement. Never a joke at the expense
of the dead; never a false fact for a laugh.
