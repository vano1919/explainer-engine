## 0. The rules of this universe (stated aloud in the video's first minute)

A thought experiment needs fixed rules, otherwise anything can "happen". These are the only exceptions to reality:

1. **Where and when.** A river valley in the foothills of southeastern Anatolia, about 12,000 years before our present.
   We chose it because in our own world this region held the wild ancestors of wheat, barley, lentils, goats, sheep,
   cattle and pigs, plus copper outcrops. The universe is "identical to Earth", so the same things grow here. **[V]**
2. **Who.** 100 people, each at the age of their most famous work, healthy, wearing the clothes they wore, plus whatever
   was in their pockets. Nothing else: no books, no tools, no notes.
3. **One gift.** They can all understand each other. Without it the video would be about translation. This is the only
   magic in the story.
4. **What they know.** What each person knew in life, and no more. Tesla does not know what a transistor is. Nobody
   remembers every constant exactly; they remember what is *possible*, and that is worth more than it sounds.
5. **Who else is here.** Scattered hunter-gatherer bands live in the wider world. None is near the valley. (This matters
   in Year 3.)

### Who they are (a sample, not the full 100)

| Group | Examples | What they bring | What they lack |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor survivors | Fridtjof Nansen, Alexander von Humboldt, Alfred Russel Wallace, Charles Darwin | Cold, hunger and field experience; plants and animals | Few have ever made a tool from scratch |

| Mechanics and engineers | James Watt, Henry Maudslay, Archimedes, Nikola Tesla, Thomas Edison, George Stephenson | Machines, tooling, power | Machines need materials that do not yet exist |

## 1. Day 1 — The Arrival and the Shock

**Hook line for the video:** "There is no one to ask. The smartest hundred people in history are standing in a meadow, and
the first question is not about physics. It is: where do we sleep?"

### What actually happens

They arrive at once, on grass, late in the morning of a late-spring day. Nobody is hurt. Everybody is talking. For about
an hour, nothing useful happens, because a hundred brilliant people all have a different idea of what is useful.

**The leadership problem.** The first useful sentence comes from the person with the most direct experience of being cold,
hungry and far from help: Fridtjof Nansen, who has crossed Greenland's ice and drifted in the Arctic. He asks one thing:
*"How many of us can make fire without matches?"* Two hands go up, and both of them are unsure.

What follows is what a real group in trouble does. It does not elect a king. It splits by **urgency** rather than by
prestige:

- **Survival first, theory later.** Nansen and the field naturalists (Humboldt, Wallace, Darwin) take charge of the
  *physical* day. Benjamin Franklin, whose life was full of committees, volunteers to run the meeting itself.
- Franklin proposes a rule that turns out to be the most important decision of the day: **every group reports to a
  nightly meeting, and any member can stop work for safety reasons.** Newton, who dislikes being told what to do, and
  Einstein, who dislikes authority in general, both agree for the same reason: nobody wants to be the person who
  voted to skip the water.

### The survival rules of three

Nansen uses the standard order of urgency for a person in the open:

| Need | How long you last | Day-1 answer |
|---|---|---|
| Staying warm and dry | Hours in cold wet wind | Windbreak, dry insulation, fire |
| Water | About 3 days | Find a spring |
| Food | Weeks | Not today |

### Water (the first success)

Wallace points out that a spring, where water leaves the ground, is safer than a pool, where animals drink and defecate.
John Snow, whose work was about exactly that, adds the rule that will save lives in all the weeks to come: **latrines
are dug downstream of camp, never upstream, and never near the spring.** They drink at the source on Day 1 and accept the
small risk. They know they cannot boil it yet, because they have no pot.

### Shelter

Late-spring nights in highland Anatolia are only a few degrees above freezing. **[V]** The danger is not the cold itself
but cold plus wind plus damp clothes, which kills by hypothermia long before it is "freezing". They build a windbreak of
branches, then pile dry grass and leaf litter against it. A "debris hut" works because trapped air is what insulates.
A hundred people sleep in clusters, tucked together, which is the oldest heating system there is.

### Fire (the hardest thing on the first day)

Everyone wants fire. Nobody has made it without matches.

- **Lens fire.** Anyone with glasses has a lens. A convex reading lens focuses sunlight into a hot point; with dry grass
  and shredded bark it can work. It works only when the sun is out, and a cloud ends the experiment.
- **Friction fire.** A hand drill or bow drill (a spindle spun into a hearth board) generates a hot dust coal. It works,
  but it is a *skill*, and the first dozen attempts fail. Humidity, wrong wood, wrong speed.
- **Spark fire.** Flint struck against pyrite or marcasite throws sparks. The sparks need a catcher: charred cloth, or
  the dry inner part of a bracket fungus. Marcasite nodules are common in chalk; finding them is geology. **[V]**

The first flame on Day 1 comes from the lens, because the sky happens to be clear. Then comes the real engineering
problem, which is not making fire but **keeping it**. Embers are carried in a bundle of bark and moss. Franklin, who
once founded a volunteer fire company, organises a fire watch with shifts, because a fire that goes out in the night costs
another day.

### Food (Day 1: mostly nothing)

A hundred adults need about 2,500 kcal a day, which is 250,000 kcal. That is a staggering amount of food for a group that
has nothing. They eat nothing on Day 1, deliberately: Darwin and Wallace refuse to gamble on unknown plants. The old
"universal edibility test" is slow and risky, and a poisoning at this point could cripple the group. They eat only what
they can recognise: some nuts, bird eggs from a nest Wallace spots, snails, and wild einkorn grains already ripening on
the hillside. The grain, which they will rely on for months, is eaten raw, and it is *barely enough*.

### End of Day 1

Result: one spring, one fire, one windbreak, and one rule (the nightly meeting). Nobody has invented anything. The whole
day was spent *copying* what a hunter-gatherer child of ten could do, and the group is humbled by it.

**Dependency chain to remember:** water → vessel → heat to boil → safe water → time and energy for everything else.

## 2. Week 1 — Organisation and Basics

### The Census

Franklin runs the first big task: an honest inventory. Not names, **skills**. The list is short and humbling.

- Hands-on survival experience: ~6 people.
- Anyone who has ever knapped flint, made rope, fired clay or cooked over an open fire: ~4.
- Anyone who has treated a wound outdoors: ~10 (the physicians, mostly).
- Anyone who understands *why* something works but has never done it: the rest.

The scenario then reorganises into **working parties of 8 to 12**, each with one practical person and several specialists.
Specialists are useful as *amplifiers*: a person who understands why a method works can improve it quickly, but only
after someone has done it once.

### The labour budget (the number that governs everything)

Wild einkorn stands can be harvested at roughly 2 kg of clean grain per hour with a stone-bladed sickle, an old
field experiment by Jack Harlan on Karacadağ in this very region. **[V]** At 3,300 kcal per kilogram of grain **[V]**:

- 250,000 kcal per day ÷ 3,300 = about **75 kg of grain a day**.
- Harvesting it: about 35 person-hours a day.
- Threshing, winnowing, grinding: far more, because hand grinding is slow. Perhaps 10 or more people full-time.

So by the end of Week 1, **roughly a third of the entire colony is busy with grain alone.** The remaining two thirds must
build shelter, gather firewood, haul water, cook, dig latrines and make tools. This is the real reason stone-age societies
looked "primitive": it is not stupidity, it is that every hour has an owner. The group's first scientific insight is
accounting.

### Tools: stone is not primitive, it is a trade

- **Flint and chert** break with a sharp, glassy edge. Geologists (Hutton, Lyell, Anning) find nodules in the limestone
  hills, because flint sits inside chalk and limestone in layers. **[V]**
- **Knapping** is learned the hard way. By day four the colony has a stock of flakes, scrapers and crude hand axes. The
  group's blades are as good as any hunter-gatherer's, but only after a week of cuts, bruises and a few lost fingers.
- **Hafting** is the real leap: a stone head on a wooden handle multiplies force. They need glue. Pine resin mixed with
  charcoal and a little beeswax becomes a mastic that holds a head to a haft. Rawhide and sinew lashings shrink as they
  dry, which tightens the joint automatically. The first hafted axe fells a small tree in about an hour. **[V]**
- **Cordage.** Rope comes from twisting plant fibres (nettle, lime-tree bast, flax). Newton and Archimedes, studying the
  twist, explain why it works: twist forces the fibres to press against each other, and friction holds the strand
  together. Every machine afterwards uses rope.

### The first machines (almost no technology required)

Newton and Archimedes design only things that can be made of wood and stone:

- **Levers and rollers** to move logs and boulders. A crowbar of a stout sapling. A log with a notch for a fulcrum.
- **A simple pulley** (a grooved wooden wheel on a pin) with the new rope: it does not reduce work but it changes the
  *direction* of the pull, so people can haul while standing on the ground.
- **A sledge** of two runners.

Archimedes sketches a more elaborate system. Newton points out that the colony cannot yet make the metal pin. They agree
on a wooden pin that wears out in a week. It is a good illustration of the whole story: *design is easy, materials are
hard.*

### Water purification (properly)

Pottery does not exist yet, so boiling water for 100 people is a real puzzle. The answer, used historically in many
cultures, is **stone boiling**. Wooden troughs and hide-lined pits are filled with water, and fist-sized rocks are heated
in the fire and dropped in. Two details matter, and both are physics:

- **Use dry, dense rock.** Wet river cobbles can have water trapped inside; heated, it turns to steam and the rock
  explodes. Several people get small cuts before Lyell works this out.
- **Boiling is the only reliable step.** Charcoal and sand filters make water clearer, and charcoal removes some
  chemicals, but filtering alone does not guarantee that germs are gone. Snow and Pasteur insist on boiling anything that
  will be drunk by the sick, the very young, or the exhausted.

### Hygiene

Semmelweis and Lister argue for the next two decisions, which look dull and save more lives than any invention:

- **Soap** from animal fat and wood-ash lye. Make it once and everyone washes their hands before eating and after the
  latrine. **[V]**
- **Wound care.** Clean with boiled water, cover with a dressing, and use honey where it can be had; honey is a
  well-documented antibacterial dressing. **[V]**

### End of week

A secure camp, a stock of tools, a safe water routine. Two people have fallen ill with diarrhoea, one has an infected cut,
and one has broken a wrist. The colony is alive, but only barely ahead of its problems.

## 3. Month 1 — The First Leap: Farming and Real Houses

### The calendar trick

Nobody has time for farming yet, because the wild grain is still being harvested. But the *knowledge* of farming is the
group's greatest advantage, and **Mendel and Borlaug** know something that took humans thousands of years: **seed from the
best plants gives better plants.**

In our history, the early farmers did this by accident. They did not know that it was selection. The ancestors of wheat,
for example, have seed heads that **shatter**: the ripe grain falls to the ground, which is how the wild plant spreads.
For a farmer, a shattering plant loses its harvest. A rare mutant with a tough rachis (the stalk holding the grain) stays
whole and is harvested with the rest. Whenever people sowed the harvested seed, they unconsciously selected for the tough
rachis. **[V]** Mendel's colony does this *on purpose*, on the first harvest: they mark the plants that hold their grain
and save only those. Borlaug, who spent a career selecting wheat, argues that the first strain does not need to be
beautiful; it needs to be *reliable*.

### Sowing plan

- Wild winter cereals are sown in autumn in this climate. They choose a **field** by the river on loose, fertile soil.
- Tools: **digging sticks** with fire-hardened points, then wooden hoes tipped with stone blades.
- **Pulses** (lentils, peas, chickpeas, bitter vetch) alongside cereals. Liebig and Carver explain why: legumes host bacteria
  in their roots that fix nitrogen, so a field that has grown beans feeds the next crop of grain. (The bacteria were
  identified only in the 1880s; the *pattern* of rotating crops was known to farmers for centuries.) **[V]**
- **Manure and ash** as fertiliser.

### Animals

- They don't have a single domestic animal. Goats and sheep are the first to be taken, in the way real early herders
  did: capture young animals and keep the tamest. Wolves can be tamed from pups; dogs are an early and cheap
  advantage (guarding, hunting).
- The aurochs (the wild ancestor of cattle) is dangerous and left for later.

### The pottery breakthrough

Clay is everywhere. But pottery is not just "shaping clay and baking it". First-time pots **crack**. The cause is physics:
wet clay shrinks as it dries and again as it fires, and uneven shrinking tears the pot. The fix is **temper**: crushed
shell, crushed calcite, sand or chopped straw mixed into the clay to hold it together. Lavoisier and Mendeleev's
chemistry explains the clay's behaviour (water is driven off around 100 °C, chemically bound water goes at around
500 to 600 °C, and above that the clay turns to a hard, ceramic material); the actual finding of a working recipe takes
about **30 failed pots** and a week.

An open fire in a pit can reach about 700 to 900 °C. **[V]** A **kiln** (a fire chamber separated from the pots, with a
chimney) reaches higher. This difference is what leads to metal.

Pots change everything: water can be boiled directly in a pot over a fire; grain can be stored sealed and rodent-proof;
food can be cooked, reducing disease and making cereal digestible. The colony's death rate falls noticeably.

### Real houses

For houses they use two materials that need no metal at all:

- **Mudbrick** (clay and straw, sun-dried) and **wattle and daub** (woven stakes plastered with clay).
- **Lime mortar and plaster.** Burn limestone at about 900 °C and you get quicklime; add water and you get slaked lime;
  mix with sand and it hardens as it takes up carbon dioxide from the air. People in this region made lime plaster
  long before pottery. **[V]** So the chemistry is easy and the *fuel* is the problem: a house worth of lime burns a
  huge pile of wood.
- **Geometry on the building site.** Right angles from a rope with 12 knots (3-4-5 triangle), a plumb line, a water
  level made of a trough. Foundations on stone. Archimedes and Gauss design a round house with a conical roof, because a
  circle uses the least wall for a given floor area. **[V]**

### Copper, the first attempt

Geologists find **green stains** on rocks in the hills: the copper carbonate mineral malachite. The route from ore to
metal needs three things: the ore, charcoal (a fuel and a reducing agent), and a temperature of about **1,085 °C**, the
melting point of copper. **[V]**

- **Charcoal.** Wood is burned slowly under a covering of earth, in a mound called a clamp, so that most of the oxygen is
  excluded. This makes a fuel that burns hotter than wood.
- **Forced air.** The fire needs a blast. First they blow through reeds; then a pair of **goatskin bellows** with a clay
  nozzle called a tuyère. A clay nozzle matters: a bare reed burns away.
- **The crucible.** A small pottery vessel that does not melt.

The first four attempts give a lumpy, green, brittle blob of slag. The fifth gives a drop the size of a pea. A week later
they pour a handful of copper into a stone mould. It is not an axe. It is a proof that the route works.

> Note for the video: the point is **not** that copper is hard, it is that each step needs the one before it. No kiln, no
> crucible; no charcoal, no heat; no bellows, no temperature.

## 4. Year 1 — The Mini Industrial Revolution

By the end of the first year, the colony is no longer a camp. It is a small village with a plan.

### The first winter (a near-miss)

The colony survives the first winter, but not comfortably. Three things nearly kill it:

1. **Not enough stored calories.** The first harvest was too small for 100 people for a whole winter plus seed grain. They
   eat the seed corn once. They then borrow from the next year's plan.
2. **Dysentery** in the wettest month, from a latrine too close to the water. Snow is furious; so is everyone else.
3. **Vitamin C.** Without fresh plants in winter, signs of scurvy appear. Pine-needle tea and dried rosehips fix it. The
   physicians know the cure from the story of James Lind's sailors; the plants are simple to find once someone is
   looking. **[V]**

In the scenario, **9 of the 100 die in Year 1**: infection, an accident with a falling log, a bad fall, and a case of
pneumonia. This is *an invented figure from the story's model*, not a statistic.

### Power: the waterwheel

The colony's real power is the river. Watt, Archimedes and Maudslay design a **waterwheel** with wooden buckets,
mounted on a wooden axle that turns in a stone bearing greased with animal fat. It is not efficient, but it does not eat.

- It drives **bellows** for the furnaces, which allows a hotter and more steady fire.
- It drives a **trip hammer**, a heavy wooden head lifted by cams and dropped, for hammering ore and hot metal.
- Next season, it drives a **grain mill** with two stone discs, the first labour saver: it frees about 10 people from
  grinding. The mill is the point at which the colony begins to have *surplus labour*.

### Metal: copper, then iron

The group tries to make **bronze** (copper plus tin). In our own world, bronze needed tin, and tin was rare. Geologists pan
the streams for heavy black grains of cassiterite (tin ore), and find a little; Anning and Hutton suspect there is
more in the mountains. **[V]** Arsenic-copper alloys are also known to have been used, and the chemists know exactly why
they refuse: smelting arsenic ores releases toxic fumes. The colony's rule is **no recipe that poisons the smelter.**

Then comes the surprise: because the group *knows* that iron is the future and knows roughly how it is made, they go
for it much earlier than history did. A **bloomery furnace**, a clay chimney with a bellows, reduces iron ore with
charcoal at about 1,200 °C, **below the melting point of iron (about 1,538 °C)**, so it produces a spongy lump called a
bloom, which must be hammered to squeeze out the slag. **[V]** The iron is soft and low in carbon. Lavoisier explains
why carbon makes it harder; Bessemer, who made his career on steel, cannot make steel yet, but he can make a *carburised
surface* by heating iron in charcoal. The first iron axe, hammered by the waterwheel's trip hammer, cuts a log three times
faster than the stone axe. That single tool changes the economics of the colony.

### Measurement

The group fights, in a friendly way, about units. Newton prefers English feet; Leibniz and Lavoisier want a rational
system. They compromise. **A unit of length is defined by a pendulum that swings once per second**, about 1 metre long
(it varies by about half a percent with latitude, so they fix it at their own valley). **[V]** A litre is a cube of water
0.1 m on a side, and a kilogram is a litre of water. Galileo, whose studies of pendulums started the whole thing, is
visibly pleased.

They also have to agree on **time**. A water clock gives hours; a pendulum clock, once a metal gear is possible, gives
minutes. A **calendar** counts from Day 1.

### Mathematics

The colony settles on a single notation. Positional decimal numbers (the system we use), zero, and a handful of symbols.
This is easy to agree on because a clear system is obviously better than Roman numerals.

### Medicine (the sober version)

- **Willow bark** for pain and fever (its active compound, salicin, is a relative of aspirin). **[V]**
- **Alcohol** for cleaning wounds, once fermentation and the first clay still are working. **[V]**
- **Surgery** is limited to cutting out dead tissue, setting bones, draining abscesses. Anaesthesia is a future step.
- **Immunisation** is not possible yet. And there is one more worry the physicians raise: **the colony itself is a disease
  carrier.** A hundred people from different centuries bring cold viruses, tuberculosis, and herpes viruses. Hunter-gatherers
  living far away have never met these germs. If the colony meets them carelessly, it may kill them. The colony votes:
  *no contact without a plan.* **[V]**

### Writing and records

Charcoal and lampblack ink, tree-bark and hide for writing, a first alphabet and number notation. The first
written record is an inventory of seeds. Within a year, an "encyclopaedia" project begins: each of the 91 survivors
writes down what they know in their field. It's less reliable than it looks. People forget details, and
two chemists remember a reaction temperature differently by 100 degrees, so each entry is marked "tested" or "remembered".
*That small label is the most scientific thing they do all year.*

## 5. Years 2–10 — Electrification and Infrastructure

### Population

The colony grows. The group is mostly adults in good health; about 40 of the women are of childbearing age. With good food,
clean water and hygiene, children survive at a much higher rate than in a typical stone-age population. By Year 10 there
are roughly **120 to 160 people**, about half of them children. This figure is an estimate from the model, **[V]**
and the story treats it as such. The colony's main problem for the next decade is **people**: 91 specialists cannot staff
a farm, a mill, a mine, a forge, a school and a hospital.

### Schools

The first school opens in Year 3, when the oldest children are about 6. The curriculum is practical: numbers, writing,
farming, a trade. In Year 5, each original scientist takes two apprentices. This is the colony's real inheritance: the
people who will do the work in Year 50 are the children now being taught.

### First contact (Year 3)

A hunting band appears at the edge of the valley. The colony has debated this for two years. They follow a plan: meet on
neutral ground, no gifts of food or clothing that carry germs, a few people only, stay at a distance, no shaking hands,
and **no one with a cold goes near**. The hunters are cautious and curious. A trade begins: obsidian for pots. Nobody
pretends this solves the ethical problem; it is simply the best plan available.

### Chemistry

The colony needs chemicals to make anything beyond wood and iron.

- **Soda and potash** from plant ash and from natural salt flats, for glass and soap.
- **Sulfuric acid** by heating green vitriol (iron sulfate), a mineral that forms when pyrite weathers; the vapour
  condensed in a glass or pottery vessel is "oil of vitriol". **[V]**
- **Nitric acid** from saltpetre (potassium nitrate) plus sulfuric acid. Saltpetre is made in "nitre beds", piles of
  manure, soil and ash left for months. **[V]**
- **Glass** from sand, ash and lime melted at about 1,000 to 1,100 °C. Glass leads to lenses, bottles, and
  eventually thermometers and a microscope that lets the physicians **see** bacteria. Pasteur, silent for four years
  because he had no way to prove what he believed, finally looks through a lens and shows a roomful of people an
  organism no larger than a speck.
- **Gunpowder** is possible (saltpetre, charcoal, sulfur). The council debates it for three weeks and votes to keep it
  locked up for mining only.

### Steel and the blast furnace

A taller furnace with a stronger, waterwheel-driven blast reaches a higher temperature and melts the iron into **cast
iron**. Cast iron is brittle but can be poured into moulds; it gives rails, gears, cannons (not made) and pipes. To make
useful steel they need to control the carbon. This takes three years of trial and error and a lot of broken tools.
Bessemer's method (blowing air through molten iron to burn off the carbon) needs a refractory lining that survives that
heat, which the colony cannot yet make. Year 10 sees **crucible steel** in small amounts: good for knives, springs and
drills. **[V]**

### Precision

A steam engine's cylinder has to be round and smooth enough for a piston to make a tight seal. In our history, James
Watt's engine waited for John Wilkinson's boring machine. **[V]** Henry Maudslay, who invented the screw-cutting lathe,
leads the effort: first a lathe, then a better lathe made by the first lathe, then a better one still. *Machines that make
machines* take about four years.

### Wire, magnets and the first electricity

Edison wants light. Tesla wants motors. Faraday wants to know what is going on. Maxwell wants to do the sums. The
problem for all of them is **wire**:

- **Wire** is made by hammering copper into rod, then pulling it through a series of smaller and smaller holes in an
  iron plate (a drawplate). The waterwheel does the pulling. It takes about two years to get thin, even wire.
- **Insulation.** Wire has to be coated or the current shorts. Silk thread, cotton thread, beeswax, varnish. The colony has
  neither silk nor cotton yet, so linen thread and varnish.
- **Magnets.** Natural lodestone (magnetite) is rare. The colony has a single piece. An iron bar rubbed with it becomes
  a weak magnet; once they have a battery they can make stronger *electro*magnets. **[V]**
- **Batteries.** Volta's pile needs copper and zinc. Zinc is tricky: its ore cannot be smelted like copper because the
  metal boils away at a lower temperature than the reaction needs. So they use copper and iron plates in brine at
  first: a weak cell, but enough to move a compass needle and show Faraday his principle. **[V]**
- **A generator.** Faraday's rotating copper disc between magnet poles gives a small current. Years of improvements later,
  a **coil dynamo** with an iron core gives enough to run an arc lamp for ten minutes at a time.

Year 10 shows a single arc lamp lighting the village square for ten minutes. Everyone watches it. Tesla and Edison do
not agree about what to build next: *direct current is easy to make; alternating current is easier to transmit*. They
compromise: DC for the workshops, AC for a later power line.

> In our world: the first telegraph was 1830s–40s, the first dynamo 1860s, and the first power stations 1880s. **[V]**

### Telegraph

Because the valley is a few kilometres long, a **telegraph** between the mine, the forge and the farm saves more labour
than electric light. It's the first electrical device the colony *uses every day*.

## 6. Year 50 — The New Era

### The survivors

In the scenario, **only six of the original 100 are still alive.** Most were in their forties or fifties on arrival. At
Year 50 they are in their nineties. Everyone else has died of age, illness or accident. The grown children who run the
colony now are the real heirs. *The people who built this world are largely the people who were taught by the people who
were taught by the originals.*

### What the society looks like

- **Population:** roughly **600 to 1,500**. **[V]** (Our model: 100 adults, 4 children per woman surviving, growth 2 to 3% a
  year. It's an estimate.)
- **Economy:** farming by plough (iron) and oxen, a mill, a forge, a cotton-and-linen workshop, a small mine for coal.
  *Most people still work in agriculture.* This is the sobering detail: even a "high-tech" colony needs most of its
  labour to produce food.
- **Power:** a few steam engines (coal-fired, high-pressure steam is possible but dangerous), many waterwheels and a
  couple of windmills.
- **Electricity:** a hydroelectric station, a village grid, lamps, a telegraph line, and a small AC line to the mine.
- **Transport:** a **railway about 30 km** long from the mine to the river, with iron rails and a **steam locomotive**
  that pulls ore cars. *It is a handful of vehicles, not a network.*
- **Medicine:** ether anaesthesia, antiseptic surgery, a vaccine against **smallpox**? **No** (the disease does not exist
  here and the vaccine is of no use). A culture of the mould *Penicillium* used for wound infections is possible but
  inconsistent. **[V]**
- **Education:** a school of 80 pupils and a library of several hundred paper volumes printed on a press.
- **Law and politics:** a council, a written constitution and plenty of arguments. Few of the original scientists
  turned out to be good rulers. The institutions that survive are those that were *boring*: records, rules and routines.

### The car

A **car** would need: refined petroleum (no oil industry), rubber tyres (natural rubber comes from a tree native to South
America, and vulcanisation was discovered in 1839), steel sheet, a spark ignition and a reliable engine, precision
machining, and roads. **[V]** Each of those is a separate industry. The colony reaches the point of **designing** a
car by Year 40. It can't build one until it has oil wells, a chemical industry and a road network; that's decades more.

> The correct way to tell this in the video: "A car is not a thing. It is the last step of about forty others."

## 7. Conclusion — The Comparison

### Their clock versus ours

| Milestone | Our history | The colony |
|---|---|---|
| Controlled fire | hundreds of thousands of years ago **[V]** | Day 1 |
| Farming and herding | ~11,500 years ago **[V]** | Year 1 |
| Pottery | earlier than farming in some places **[V]** | Month 1 |
| Copper smelting | ~7,000 years ago **[V]** | Month 1–2 |
| Iron smelting | ~3,200 years ago **[V]** | Year 1 |
| Steam engine | 1712 (Newcomen); 1769 (Watt) **[V]** | ~Year 25 |
| Electric telegraph | 1830s–40s **[V]** | ~Year 12 |
| Steam locomotive | 1804 (first trial); 1825 (public railway) **[V]** | ~Year 40 |

That is about **12,000 years of real history compressed into 50**: a speed-up on the order of a few hundred times.

### What explains the speed-up

1. **Knowing that something is possible.** The hardest part of any invention is the belief that it can be done. These
   people knew iron could be made, so they went directly to iron.
2. **Knowing what *not* to do.** They skip dead ends: they skip the poisonous alloys, skip the long search for a hardness
   standard, and use hygiene from Day 1. Nobody in history ever had that.
3. **Specialists in every field**, so each problem has someone who understands the physics.
4. **Accounting.** Scientists treat labour, fuel and time as quantities to be budgeted.

### What it does *not* explain away

- They cannot skip **the materials**: ore, clay, wood, water and fuel all have to be found and processed.
- They cannot skip **the chain**: each tool is made by the one before it.
- They cannot skip **the people**: 100 is a tiny population, and most of its hours go to eating.
- They cannot skip **luck**: the model has the colony survive. Many runs of the same experiment would end in the first
  winter, in an epidemic, or in a quarrel that splits the group.

### The closing line (draft)

"Knowledge is not a shortcut around the work. It is a map of the work. The hundred did not skip the Stone Age. They
walked through it with the lights on."
