What happens if you drop the greatest minds in history into the Stone Age? No tools. No matches. And night is coming fast.
Here are the rules. A hundred scientists. A river valley in southeastern Anatolia, twelve thousand years ago. Each one arrives at the age of their most famous work, in their own clothes, with only what's in their pockets. They can all understand each other. That's the only magic. The rocks, the plants, the physics — all painfully real. The scientists are real people. What they say and do here is our invention. So who takes charge? And who panics first?
Day one, late morning. A hundred brilliant people, and a hundred different ideas of what to do first. For a whole hour, nothing useful happens. Then Fridtjof Nansen — the man who crossed Greenland on skis — asks the one question that matters. How many of you can make fire without matches? Two hands go up. Halfway. Both of them... unsure.
Benjamin Franklin takes over the meeting. Naturally. He sets one rule: every group reports at a nightly meeting, and anyone can stop work for safety. Even Newton agrees. Somebody write that down. But a rule won't keep anyone warm. And the sun is already sinking.
The order is simple. Warmth first. Then water. Then food. Alfred Russel Wallace finds a spring, right where the water leaves the ground. And John Snow — yes, that John Snow — adds the rule that will save lives for months: latrines go downstream. Never near the spring. Then the wind changes.
As the sun drops, the wind picks up, and the real danger arrives. It isn't freezing. It's cold, wind and damp clothes, all at once. So they build a windbreak of branches, and pile dry grass against it. Trapped air is the oldest insulation there is. It buys them time. But only fire buys them the night.
Now. Fire. Nansen tries a bow drill. A dozen tries, sore hands, and nothing but smoke. Franklin keeps the tinder dry, and waits. Then Einstein takes off his reading glasses. A convex lens. The afternoon sun. One bright point on the grass. A thread of smoke. One careful breath... Fire!
The hard part isn't making it. It's keeping it. Franklin, who once founded a volunteer fire company, sets a night watch, in shifts.
Dinner is almost nothing: a few nuts, some eggs, raw wild grain. Darwin and Wallace refuse to gamble on plants they don't know. Wise. Because out here, one wrong guess can poison you.
One spring. One fire. One windbreak. One rule. Nobody has invented anything. They spent the whole day doing what a ten-year-old hunter-gatherer could do. Slower. And with more opinions. And tomorrow? Tomorrow, they need a pot.
