Could history's greatest minds survive the Stone Age? No tools. No matches. And night is coming.
Here are the rules. A hundred scientists. A river valley in southeastern Anatolia, twelve thousand years ago. Each one arrives at the age of their most famous work, in their own clothes, with only what was in their pockets. They can all understand each other. That is the only magic. The rocks, the plants and the physics are real. The scientists are real people too, but what they say and do here is our invention.
Day one, late morning. A hundred brilliant people, and a hundred different ideas of what to do first. For an hour, nothing useful happens. Then Fridtjof Nansen, who crossed Greenland on skis, asks one question. How many of us can make fire without matches? Two hands go up. Both of them are unsure.
Benjamin Franklin takes over the meeting, and sets one rule. Every group reports at a nightly meeting, and anyone can stop work for safety. Even Newton agrees.
The order is simple: warmth first, then water, then food. Alfred Russel Wallace finds a spring, where the water leaves the ground. John Snow adds the rule that will save lives for months. Latrines go downstream, never near the spring.
As the sun drops, the wind picks up, and the real danger arrives. Not freezing, but cold, wind and damp clothes together. So they build a windbreak of branches, and pile dry grass against it. Trapped air is the oldest insulation there is.
Now, fire. Nansen tries a bow drill. A dozen tries, sore hands, and nothing but smoke. Franklin keeps the tinder dry, and waits. Then Einstein takes off his reading glasses. A convex lens, the afternoon sun, and one bright point on the grass. A thread of smoke. One careful breath. Fire.
The hard part is not making it. It is keeping it. Franklin, who once founded a volunteer fire company, sets a night watch in shifts.
They eat almost nothing: a few nuts, some eggs, raw wild grain. Darwin and Wallace refuse to gamble on plants they do not know.
One spring. One fire. One windbreak. One rule. Nobody has invented anything. They spent the whole day doing what a ten-year-old hunter-gatherer could do. And tomorrow, they need a pot.
