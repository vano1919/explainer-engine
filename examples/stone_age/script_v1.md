## 2. Week 1 — Organisation and basics (≈ 2:34–4:20) — palette `week1` — dissolve in (time jump)

**Beat 2.1 — the census (A → B → C).**
> One week later. Franklin's first big job is an honest inventory, not of names, but of skills. It is short, and it is
> humbling. About six of the hundred have real survival experience. About four have ever knapped flint, made rope, or
> fired clay. Everyone else knows why things work, and has never done them. [model]

- A, calm: the camp from the hillside one week on, the windbreak grown into a row of shelters, tiny figures.
- B, humour: Franklin (caricature) with a charcoal stick and a flat bark slab, counting.
- C, tension: a hand tallying marks on bark; the column for "has done it" is almost empty.

**Beat 2.2 — the labour budget (A → C → B → C).**
> So they reorganise into working parties of eight to twelve, each led by one practical person. Then they meet the number
> that rules everything. A hundred adults need about two hundred and fifty thousand calories a day. Wild einkorn can be
> harvested at about two kilograms an hour with a stone sickle [C 4]. That is about seventy-five kilograms of grain a day
> [C 5], and grinding it takes far longer than cutting it. By the end of the week, a third of the colony works on grain
> alone. Every hour has an owner.

- A, tension: a golden hillside of wild grain with a long line of tiny bent figures.
- C, tension: a stone-bladed sickle cutting a fistful of grain stalks.
- B, tension: a faceless figure kneeling at a saddle quern, grinding (from behind).
- C, discovery: a small heap of flour on the quern stone, a hand scraping it together.

**Beat 2.3 — stone is a trade (A → B → C → C).**
> Tools come next, and stone turns out to be a trade, not a primitive. The geologists find flint nodules in the limestone
> [C 6]. Knapping is learned the hard way: by day four there is a stock of flakes, scrapers and crude hand axes, and a
> great many cut fingers. The real leap is hafting. Pine resin mixed with charcoal glues a stone head to a wooden handle,
> and sinew lashings shrink as they dry, pulling the joint tight [C 7].

- A, discovery: a limestone outcrop above the valley, white nodules in the rock face, two tiny climbers.
- B, tension: Newton (caricature) wincing at a sharp flake; a plate of the knapping floor.
- C, tension: hands striking flint with a hammerstone, flakes flying.
- C, discovery: a hafted axe head lashed with sinew, resin glistening.

**Beat 2.4 — machines of wood (B → C → A).**
> Newton and Archimedes design only what wood and stone allow: levers, rollers, a sledge, a grooved wooden pulley that
> changes the direction of a pull. Archimedes sketches something grander. Newton points out they cannot make the metal pin.
> Design is easy. Materials are hard.

- B, humour: Archimedes (caricature) drawing a pulley system in the dirt; plate of a log yard.
- C, humour: a wooden pin snapping in a pulley block.
- A, calm: a log rolled on rollers across the meadow by a tiny team.

**Beat 2.5 — boiling without a pot, and soap (A → C → C → B → C).**
> There is still no pot, so they boil water with stones: rocks heated in the fire and dropped into a hide-lined pit. Wet
> river cobbles can explode when heated, which the colony learns from several small cuts. Boiling is the only reliable
> step; a charcoal filter makes water clearer, but not safe. Semmelweis and Lister insist on soap from animal fat and
> wood-ash lye [C 8], and on honey for wounds [C 9]. At the end of the week: a secure camp, a stock of tools, a water
> routine. Two people are sick, one cut is infected, one wrist is broken. They are alive, but only just ahead.

- A, tension: a pit camp with steam rising from a hide-lined hole, tiny figures around.
- C, fear: a river cobble cracking in the fire, a spray of fragments.
- C, discovery: a glowing stone dropped into water, a burst of steam.
- B, calm: a faceless figure washing hands in a wooden trough, grey suds.
- C, sorrow: a bandaged wrist resting on a knee, firelight.

## 3. Month 1 — Farming, pots and the first copper (≈ 4:20–6:25) — palette `month1` — dissolve in

**Beat 3.1 — selection on purpose (A → C → B).**
> One month in, the colony's greatest advantage is not a machine. It is an idea. Wild wheat shatters: the ripe grain falls
> to the ground. A rare plant with a tough stalk keeps its grain, and the first farmers in history selected it without
> knowing [C 10]. Mendel and Borlaug do it on purpose. They mark the plants that hold their grain, and save only those.

- A, discovery: a valley field at harvest, a few stakes with cloth markers among the stalks, tiny figures.
- C, discovery: two grain heads side by side, one shattered and empty, one whole.
- B, joy: a faceless figure tying a cloth marker to a tall stalk (Mendel has no caricature: UR-06 else-branch).

**Beat 3.2 — the field and the beans (B → C → A).**
> They sow by the river with digging sticks, and plant lentils and peas beside the grain. Liebig and Carver explain why:
> legumes host bacteria in their roots that feed nitrogen to the next crop [C 11].

- B, calm: two faceless figures with fire-hardened digging sticks.
- C, discovery: a pulled pea plant, small pale root nodules on its roots.
- A, calm: the river field, neat rows, evening light.

**Beat 3.3 — the pottery breakthrough (C → B → C → A).**
> Clay is everywhere, and the first pots crack. Wet clay shrinks as it dries and again as it fires, and uneven shrinking
> tears the pot. The fix is temper: crushed shell, sand or straw mixed into the clay. It takes about thirty failed pots and
> a week. An open pit fire reaches about seven to nine hundred degrees [C 12]. Pots change everything: water boils in
> them, grain is stored in them, food is cooked in them. The death rate falls.

- C, sorrow: a cracked pot in the ash, the crack running through it.
- B, tension: Curie (caricature) sorting temper (shell, sand, straw) on a flat stone; plate of a clay pit.
- C, joy: a whole pot lifted out of the embers with two sticks.
- A, joy: a row of pots by the shelters at dusk, steam rising from one over a fire.

**Beat 3.4 — houses of mud and lime (A → B → C).**
> Houses come next: mudbrick, and woven walls plastered with clay. Lime plaster is older than pottery in this region
> [C 13], but burning the limestone eats a mountain of firewood. Archimedes and Gauss design a round house, because a
> circle uses the least wall for its floor.

- A, calm: a cluster of new round houses, conical roofs, the valley behind.
- B, humour: Archimedes (caricature) holding a knotted rope, a plate of a building site.
- C, discovery: a rope pegged into a 3-4-5 triangle on the ground.

**Beat 3.5 — copper, the first attempt (A → C → C → B → C).**
> Then the geologists find green stains on the rocks: malachite, a copper ore. Copper needs about one thousand and
> eighty-five degrees [C 14]: charcoal made under a mound of earth, a blast of air from goatskin bellows through a clay
> nozzle, and a crucible that does not melt. The first four tries give green, brittle slag. The fifth gives a drop the
> size of a pea. It is not an axe. It is proof that the route works.

- A, discovery: a hillside of grey rock streaked with green, two tiny figures.
- C, tension: hands working goatskin bellows, the clay nozzle glowing.
- C, sorrow: a crumbly green slag lump in an open palm.
- B, tension: a faceless smith from behind at a glowing furnace pit (no metal-trade caricature).
- C, joy: a single bead of copper shining in a broken crucible.

## 4. Year 1 — Winter, waterwheels and iron (≈ 6:25–8:35) — palette `year1` — dissolve in

**Beat 4.1 — the first winter (A → C → B → C).**
> The first winter nearly ends the experiment. The harvest is too small, and they eat the seed grain once. A latrine dug
> too close to the water brings dysentery, and John Snow is furious. Without fresh plants, signs of scurvy appear, until
> pine-needle tea and dried rosehips fix it [C 17]. In our story, nine of the hundred die in the first year. That number is
> our model, not history [model].

- A, fear: the valley under snow, a thin line of smoke from the huts, night coming.
- C, fear: an empty grain jar, a hand scraping its bottom.
- B, sorrow: a faceless figure wrapped in hides at a doorway, snow blowing in.
- C, calm: hands pouring pine-needle tea into a clay cup, steam.

**Beat 4.2 — the waterwheel (A → B → C → C).**
> Spring brings the colony's real power: the river. Watt, Archimedes and Maudslay build a waterwheel on a wooden axle in a
> stone bearing. It is not efficient, but it does not eat. It drives bellows, then a trip hammer, and the next season, a
> grain mill that frees about ten people from grinding. That is the moment the colony starts to have spare hands.

- A, joy: the river with a big wooden waterwheel turning, spray in the light, tiny figures on the bank.
- B, joy: Archimedes (caricature) pointing at the wheel's buckets; a plate of the mill race.
- C, discovery: a wooden cam lifting a trip hammer head.
- C, joy: flour pouring from between two millstones.

**Beat 4.3 — iron (A → C → B → C).**
> Bronze needs tin, and tin is rare [C 15, disputed: must check]. So the group goes straight for iron, thousands of years
> early, because they know it can be done. A clay bloomery furnace with a bellows reaches about twelve hundred degrees,
> below iron's melting point of about fifteen hundred and thirty-eight [C 14]. What comes out is a spongy bloom that must
> be hammered to squeeze out the slag. The first iron axe cuts a log three times faster than the stone one.

- A, tension: a tall clay chimney furnace at night, sparks pouring from its top, tiny silhouettes.
- C, tension: a glowing spongy bloom pulled out with wooden tongs.
- B, tension: a faceless smith from behind hammering the bloom under the trip hammer (Bessemer has no caricature).
- C, joy: an iron axe biting deep into a log.

**Beat 4.4 — metres, writing, and "tested" (B → C → A).**
> They argue about units, and settle on a length set by a pendulum that swings once a second, about one metre [C 16]. A
> litre is a cube of water a tenth of a metre on a side. Then writing: an alphabet, numbers with a zero, and an
> encyclopaedia where every entry is marked "tested" or "remembered". That small label is the most scientific thing they
> do all year.

- B, humour: Newton (caricature) timing a stone on a string; plate of a workshop.
- C, discovery: a hand writing a word next to a small sign for "tested" on bark paper.
- A, calm: the village from above in early autumn, the wheel turning, houses and fields.

## 5. Years 2–10 — Glass, wire and the first light (≈ 8:35–10:30) — palette `years2_10` — dissolve in

**Beat 5.1 — people (A → B → C).**
> By year ten there are roughly a hundred and twenty to a hundred and sixty people, about half of them children. That is
> our model's estimate [C 24, model]. A school opens in year three, and every scientist takes two apprentices. The people
> who will do the work in year fifty are the children being taught now.

- A, joy: the village grown, children running between houses, tiny.
- B, joy: Curie (caricature) at a board with chalk; plate of a schoolroom with benches.
- C, calm: a child's hand copying letters on a slate (hands only).

**Beat 5.2 — first contact, carefully (A → B → C).**
> In year three, a hunting band appears at the valley's edge. The colony has planned for this for two years: meet on
> neutral ground, a few people only, no gifts of food or clothing, and no one with a cold goes near [C 19]. A trade
> begins: obsidian for pots.

- A, tension: two small groups facing each other across a wide meadow, far apart.
- B, tension: a faceless colonist from behind, setting a pot down on a rock.
- C, discovery: a black obsidian core and a clay pot side by side on the rock.

**Beat 5.3 — glass and the hidden world (C → B → C).**
> Glass needs sand, ash and lime melted at about a thousand degrees. Glass gives bottles, lenses, and at last a microscope.
> Pasteur, silent for years without a way to prove what he believed, finally shows a roomful of people a living speck.

- C, discovery: a gob of molten glass glowing on an iron rod.
- B, discovery: a faceless figure from behind bent over a brass microscope at a window (Pasteur has no caricature).
- C, discovery: a lens over a drop of water, tiny shapes inside it.

**Beat 5.4 — wire, magnets, electricity (A → C → B → C → A).**
> Edison wants light. Tesla wants motors. Faraday wants to know what is going on. Their problem is wire: copper hammered
> into rod and pulled through smaller and smaller holes, two years of work. A copper and iron cell in brine moves a compass
> needle [C 23]. A spinning coil between magnets becomes a dynamo. In year ten, a single arc lamp lights the village square
> for ten minutes, and everyone comes to watch. Tesla and Edison still argue about what comes next.

- A, discovery: a workshop by the river at night, one window glowing, tiny figures.
- C, tension: copper wire being drawn through a drawplate, the wire thin and bright.
- B, humour: Tesla (caricature) and Einstein (caricature) by a compass on a bench; plate of the workshop.
- C, discovery: a compass needle swinging beside a coil of wire.
- A, joy: the village square at night, one brilliant arc lamp, a crowd of tiny figures in its light.

## 6. Year 50 — The new era (≈ 10:30–11:40) — palette `year50` — dissolve in

**Beat 6.1 — the survivors (A → C).**
> Fifty years later, only six of the original hundred are still alive [model]. The colony is run by the people they taught,
> and by the people those people taught.

- A, sorrow: a hillside of simple grave stones above the town at sunset.
- C, calm: an old hand resting on a cane, a younger hand on top of it.

**Beat 6.2 — what fifty years buys (A → B → C → A).**
> Roughly six hundred to fifteen hundred people [C 24, model]. Iron ploughs and oxen, mills, a forge, a coal mine. A
> hydroelectric station, a village grid, a telegraph. And a railway about thirty kilometres long, with a steam locomotive
> pulling ore cars. It is a handful of vehicles, not a network. And most people still work on the land.

- A, joy: a small steam locomotive crossing a stone bridge in the valley, smoke trailing, tiny.
- B, calm: a faceless farmer from behind behind an iron plough and two oxen.
- C, discovery: a telegraph key under a hand, a spark at the contact.
- A, calm: the lit town at dusk, lamps along one street, fields all around.

**Beat 6.3 — the car (C → B → C).**
> And a car? They design one by year forty. They cannot build it. It needs oil wells, a chemical industry, rubber from a
> tree that grows on another continent, and roads [C 27]. A car is not a thing. It is the last step of about forty others.

- C, humour: a drawing of a car on paper, pinned flat under four stones.
- B, humour: a faceless young engineer from behind, hands on hips, staring at the drawing.
- C, discovery: a chain of small objects in a row: ore, coal, glass, wire, a gear.

## 7. Conclusion — Their clock and ours (≈ 11:40–12:30) — palette `year50` (cooling to `day1` at the end)

**Beat 7.1 — the comparison (A → C → B).**
> Fire, farming, pottery, copper, iron, the telegraph, the locomotive: our species took about twelve thousand years to
> walk that road [C 28]. The hundred did it in fifty. Not because they skipped the work. Because they knew what was
> possible, and what not to try. And they could not skip the materials, the chain of tools, the number of people, or
> luck. Many runs of this experiment end in the first winter.

- A, calm: two timelines painted as two rivers, one long and winding, one short and straight, meeting the sea.
- C, sorrow: the empty grain jar from the first winter, now dusty on a shelf.
- B, discovery: a faceless figure from behind looking up at the arc lamp on the square.

**Beat 7.2 — the closing line (C → A).**
> Knowledge is not a shortcut around the work. It is a map of the work. The hundred did not skip the Stone Age. They
> walked through it with the lights on.

- C, discovery: the same hand in a tweed sleeve as the first shot, now holding a lit lamp over the mud.
- A, calm: the valley at night from the hill of Day 1, the town lit below, the first windbreak's place marked by a stone.
